Calorie deficit: what it is, why it matters and how CalorIA Scan helps you track it

To lose weight you have to eat less energy than you burn. Here's how it works, why a moderate deficit works better, and how to keep count without obsessing.

What a calorie deficit is

Your body burns energy all day: keeping its basic functions running, moving, even digesting. If you eat less energy than you burn, you're in a calorie deficit and your body draws on its reserves. If you eat what you burn, you maintain your weight; if you eat more, you're in a surplus.

That's why we talk about calories: not because they're the only thing that matters, but because they're the unit used to measure that balance.

Why it matters

To lose weight, your eating plan has to cut calories. NIDDK, the NIH institute that studies nutrition and weight, describes a reduced-calorie eating and drinking plan that is backed by science and tailored to your health, preferences and culture. And if your goal isn't to lose but to maintain, the same math helps you not overshoot.

The problem is that almost nobody knows how much they really eat. A latte can add 120 kcal without you noticing (118 of them from the milk), and a tahini dip of just 20 g brings 60 kcal. Measuring isn't obsessing: it's no longer guessing.

How big should your deficit be?

More is not better. The CDC says people who lose weight at a gradual, steady pace, about 1 to 2 pounds a week (roughly 0.5 to 1 kg), are more likely to keep it off. NIDDK recommends an initial goal of losing 5% to 10% of your starting weight within 6 months: for example, between 4 and 8 kg if you weigh 80 kg today.

You may have heard that cutting 500 calories a day equals one pound a week (the 3,500-calories-per-pound rule). It's a simplification: NIH researchers Kevin Hall and Carson Chow explain that its most serious error is ignoring that energy balance changes as you lose weight, so it overestimates the loss. Treat it as a rough idea, not a promise.

How to estimate your calories

There's no universal number: it depends on your age, size, activity and body. NIDDK's Body Weight Planner is a tool for adults 18 and older: you enter your information, a goal weight and a timeframe, and it shows how many calories to eat each day to get there and how many to maintain afterward. It isn't for people under 18 or for pregnancy and breastfeeding, and its values can come out too high if your metabolism is abnormally low or you are very sedentary.

These are always estimates. The best move is to fine-tune them with your doctor or dietitian.

When to talk to a professional first

NIDDK recommends that the first step be talking to a health care professional about your weight: they can review your medical conditions and the medicines or supplements you take, which can affect your weight or your ability to lose it.

All the more so if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, under 18, living with diabetes or another medical condition, or have a history of eating disorders. In those cases, a calorie deficit is not something to do on your own.

How to do it without obsessing

  • Start by only logging: the CDC suggests writing down everything you eat and drink for a few days to see where you can make small changes.
  • Don't forget liquids: drinks add up too. We covered it in the calories you drink.
  • Watch your protein: it helps you feel full. Here's how to track it.
  • Look at your week, not a single day: one day off your goal doesn't define anything; the average does.
  • Make small changes: the ones you can keep for months beat one extreme change.

The practical takeaway

A calorie deficit works when it's moderate, measured and sustainable. Start by logging a few days without changing anything, set a reasonable goal —ideally with a professional— and review your week. And if what you want is to maintain your weight, the same math helps you not overshoot.
