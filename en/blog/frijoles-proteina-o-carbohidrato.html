Are beans a protein or a carb? The answer, with numbers

It's one of the fastest-growing nutrition questions in Mexican searches. Beans are both, and knowing how much of each they give you helps you build a better plate.

By CalorIA Scan · November 11, 2026 · 4 min read

The short version

  • They're both: a cup of cooked black beans has about 41 g of carbs (15 of them fiber) and 15 g of protein, according to the USDA.
  • By calories, carbs weigh more: about 7 in 10 calories in beans come from carbs, and nearly 3 in 10 from protein.
  • That's why dietary guides treat them separately: in Mexico's 2023 healthy plate, legumes have their own group, and the U.S. guide counts them as a vegetable or a protein.
  • With tortillas they make a team: corn and beans complement each other in the amino acids each one lacks.

The short answer: both

Beans are a legume, and legumes don't fit in a single box. According to the USDA, 100 g of cooked black beans has about 132 kcal, 23.7 g of carbohydrate (8.7 of it fiber), 8.9 g of protein and barely half a gram of fat. A cup (172 g) is about 227 kcal, 41 g of carbs, 15 g of fiber and 15 g of protein.

Looked at by calories, about 7 in 10 come from carbohydrate and nearly 3 in 10 from protein. Strictly speaking, beans provide more carbs than protein. But they aren't just any carb: a good share is fiber, the nutrient almost nobody gets enough of.

Why dietary guides set them apart

In the new Plato del Bien Comer, the healthy plate of Mexico's 2023 dietary guidelines, legumes have their own group, separate from grains and from animal foods.

In MyPlate, the U.S. guide, beans, peas and lentils count in two groups: as a vegetable and as a protein. Its rule is practical: if you already get enough protein from other foods, count them as a vegetable; if you're short, count them as protein.

How they compare with other proteins

Using USDA data and the CalorIA Scan database, this is what protein looks like in typical portions:

  • ½ cup of pot beans (86 g, one cupped palm): about 7.6 g of protein and 114 kcal.
  • ½ cup of cooked lentils (99 g): about 8.9 g of protein and 115 kcal.
  • 1 large egg (50 g): about 6.3 g of protein and 72 kcal.
  • 30 g of queso fresco: about 6 g of protein and 70 kcal.
  • 90 g of roasted chicken breast (one palm): about 28 g of protein and 149 kcal.
  • 1 corn tortilla (30 g): 1 g of protein and 50 kcal.

The difference is density: per 100 kcal, chicken breast gives about 19 g of protein; egg, about 9; beans, about 7. Beans add protein, but to reach the same amount you need more calories than with chicken or eggs.

Refried beans vary by recipe, because lard or oil adds fat. For reference, in the CalorIA Scan database a cup of refried beans (180 g) comes in at 200 kcal, 10 g of protein and 6 g of fat.

Tortillas and beans: a team with history

Corn protein is low in two essential amino acids, lysine and tryptophan. Bean protein has plenty of both, but it's low in sulfur amino acids such as methionine, which corn does provide. That's why, as the FAO explains, they complement each other: the mix has better-quality protein than either one alone.

There's no need to obsess over combining them in every bite, either. The U.S. Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics calls the terms "complete" and "incomplete" protein misleading, and notes that well-planned vegetarian diets usually meet protein needs when enough calories are eaten.

So how should I count them?

  • If you eat meat, chicken, fish or eggs at most meals: count beans as your legume and fiber portion, not your main protein.
  • If you eat little meat: beans, lentils and chickpeas can be among your main protein sources; pair them with tortillas or rice, and with eggs or dairy when you can.
  • Measure the portion: half a cup of cooked beans fits in one cupped palm.
  • Watch what comes with them: cheese, cream, chorizo or tortilla chips push the calories up.

How much protein do you need? A healthy adult, about 0.8 g per kilo of body weight a day. We explain it in why it's worth tracking your protein.

The practical takeaway

Beans are carbohydrate and protein at the same time, with lots of fiber and almost no fat when they're cooked plain. They don't replace chicken or eggs gram for gram, but they're a good source of plant protein, especially with tortillas. Count them, measure the portion and make the most of them.
